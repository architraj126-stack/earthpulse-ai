# Climate Science: The Greenhouse Effect and Planetary Energy Balance

## Fundamental Physics
The Earth's climate system is driven by incoming solar radiation ($S_0 \approx 1361 \text{ W/m}^2$). Accounting for planetary geometry and Earth's planetary albedo ($\alpha \approx 0.30$), the average incoming shortwave solar energy absorbed by Earth is:
$$F_{\text{in}} = \frac{S_0 (1 - \alpha)}{4} \approx 238 \text{ W/m}^2$$

In radiative equilibrium without an atmosphere, the Stefan-Boltzmann law would dictate an effective blackbody emitting temperature ($T_e$):
$$T_e = \left(\frac{F_{\text{in}}}{\sigma}\right)^{1/4} \approx 255 \text{ K} \; (-18^\circ\text{C})$$

However, the actual global mean surface temperature is approximately $288 \text{ K} \; (+15^\circ\text{C})$. This $33^\circ\text{C}$ difference is the natural greenhouse effect.

## Greenhouse Gases (GHGs)
Atmospheric trace gases with three or more atoms possess vibrational and rotational quantum modes that absorb and emit longwave infrared radiation (4–50 µm):
1. **Water Vapor ($H_2O$)**: The most abundant natural greenhouse gas; acts as a powerful thermodynamic feedback rather than a direct primary forcing.
2. **Carbon Dioxide ($CO_2$)**: Responsible for roughly 66% of the enhanced greenhouse effect radiative forcing from long-lived GHGs. Atmospheric concentration has risen from pre-industrial levels of ~280 ppm to over 420 ppm today.
3. **Methane ($CH_4$)**: 100-year Global Warming Potential (GWP) is ~28 times higher than $CO_2$.
4. **Nitrous Oxide ($N_2O$)** and **Fluorinated Gases**.

## Radiative Forcing Mechanism
Elevated concentrations of GHGs increase the optical depth of the atmosphere in thermal infrared absorption bands, raising the effective altitude from which infrared radiation escapes to space. Because the troposphere cools with altitude (environmental lapse rate $\Gamma \approx 6.5^\circ\text{C/km}$), radiation to space is emitted at colder, less intense temperatures, creating an energy imbalance ($F_{\text{in}} > F_{\text{out}}$). The planet warms until surface temperatures increase enough to restore radiative equilibrium.
